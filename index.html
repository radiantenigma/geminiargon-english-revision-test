Paper 1 Architecture & Rubric

Paper 1 accounts for 35% of your total IB English SL grade. It assesses your ability to analyze unseen non-literary texts under timed conditions.

75m

1 Hour 15 Minutes

You receive 5 minutes of silent reading time followed by 75 minutes of writing time. You are presented with two unseen non-literary texts and must write an analysis on one of them.

GQ

The Guiding Question

Each text includes a formal Guiding Question focusing on a central formal or thematic aspect (e.g., "How do text and image work together to persuade..."). Your thesis must directly answer this prompt.

20

20 Marks Total

Evaluated evenly across 4 distinct rubric criteria (5 marks each). Equal weight is given to what the text means, how it constructs meaning, how you organize your essay, and your academic register.

Criterion A 5 Marks

Understanding & Interpretation

Demonstrate perceptive understanding of literal content and nuanced subtext, context, audience, and purpose. Support claims with precise, embedded textual references.

Criterion B 5 Marks

Analysis & Evaluation

Move beyond feature-spotting. Insightfully evaluate how linguistic, structural, and visual choices synergize to shape meaning and manipulate the target audience.

Criterion C 5 Marks

Focus & Organization

Maintain a razor-sharp focus on the Guiding Question. Organize paragraphs around conceptual arguments rather than a mere checklist of literary devices.

Criterion D 5 Marks

Language & Register

Write with a fluent, formal academic register, varied syntax, analytical verbs (e.g., underscores, vilifies, juxtaposes), and accurate metalanguage.

Analytical Strategies & Frameworks

Switch between the 6 core strategic tabs below to master deconstructing texts, analyzing visual media, structuring paragraphs, and managing your 75 minutes.

Best Used For: Rapid initial annotation during the first 10 minutes of the exam and crafting a watertight 4-sentence Introduction.

T

Tone & Theme

What is the writer's attitude toward the subject (Tone) and the overarching conceptual idea (Theme)?

Pro-Tip: Always use two adjectives to capture tonal complexity or tonal shifts (e.g., "shifts from sardonic and accusatory to urgently optimistic").
C

Context

Check the provenance box at the bottom of the exam paper! Note the author, date, publication, and genre conventions.

Pro-Tip: Connect the date/medium to cultural anxieties (e.g., "Published in 2023 amidst rising post-pandemic digital burnout...").
A

Audience

Never write "the general public." Narrow down by age, values, socio-economic status, and shared beliefs.

Pro-Tip: Distinguish between a primary audience (e.g., affluent urban commuters) and a secondary audience (e.g., policymakers).
M

Message / Meaning

What specific argument or ideological takeaway is the creator advancing about the theme?

Pro-Tip: Express Message as a complete clause (e.g., "that unbridled algorithmic convenience erodes human autonomy").
P

Purpose

Why does this text exist? Most Paper 1 texts have a dual purpose: an informative surface and a persuasive core.

Pro-Tip: Frame as: "To [inform/satirize/expose] X in order to [persuade/mobilize/guilt] the reader into Y."
S

Structure & Style

How is the text built (macro-progression, layout) and what linguistic/visual choices populate it?

Pro-Tip: Group stylistic choices into 3 conceptual clusters to form your 3 body paragraphs.

Best Used For: Prose non-fiction texts with a strong voice—such as Speeches, Opinion Columns, Open Letters, Memoirs, and Manifestos.

S — Speaker
Who is the voice behind the text? What persona do they construct? Do they position themselves as a humble fellow citizen, an outraged whistleblower, a seasoned expert, or an ironic observer? How do they build ethos?
O — Occasion
What is the exigence (the immediate spark or crisis) that prompted the writer to speak now? Distinguish between the immediate occasion (e.g., a UN Youth Climate Summit) and the larger occasion (decades of institutional legislative inaction).
A — Audience
To whom is this rhetoric tailored? What biases, anxieties, or moral values does the speaker assume the audience holds? Look at how inclusive pronouns ("we", "our") or direct address ("you") position the listener.
P — Purpose
What concrete change in thought, feeling, or action is the speaker attempting to engineer in the audience? What is the call to action?
S — Subject
What is the core topic, and how does the speaker frame it? For instance, is technology framed as an liberating tool or an insidious addiction?
Tone
How do diction, syntax, and figurative imagery reveal the speaker's emotional inflection? Track where the tone pivots (the volta) to mirror the rhetorical arc of the speech or op-ed.

Best Used For: Ensuring holistic rubric coverage across all text types by cross-checking the classic IB "Big 5" analytical pillars.

Pillar 1

Audience & Purpose

The anchor of your essay. Every single analytical claim in your body paragraphs must link back to how a choice affects this specific audience to achieve this specific purpose.

Pillar 2

Content & Theme

What happens on the surface vs. what is implied beneath. Analyze what perspectives are foregrounded and what inconvenient truths are omitted or marginalized.

Pillar 3

Tone & Mood

Tone = Author's attitude (e.g., didactic, reverent, scathing). Mood = Atmosphere felt by the reader (e.g., claustrophobic, urgent, nostalgic).

Pillar 4

Stylistic Devices

The micro-level choices: semantic fields, figurative tropes, modality, rhetorical appeals, register, and visual iconography.

Pillar 5

Structure & Layout

The macro-level architecture: visual hierarchy, Z-pattern eye path, panel transitions, paragraphing, and syntactic pacing.

Best Used For: Print Advertisements, Charity Appeals, Infographics, Magazine Covers, Webpages, and Comic Strips / Graphic Narratives.

1. Camera Angles & Gaze

  • Low Angle: Camera looks up at subject — confers authority, dominance, heroism, or menace.
  • High Angle: Camera looks down — renders subject vulnerable, powerless, diminutive, or trapped.
  • Eye-Level: Creates egalitarian parity, relatability, and democratic sincerity.
  • Demand Gaze: Subject stares directly out at viewer, breaking the fourth wall to demand emotional accountability.
  • Offer Gaze: Subject looks away, turning them into an object of contemplation for the viewer.

2. Framing & Composition

  • Rule of Thirds: Placing salient focal points along grid intersections to generate dynamic visual tension.
  • Center-Stage: Placing the subject dead-center to command immediate, inescapable salience.
  • Negative / White Space: Empty space around a subject that isolates it, evokes loneliness, or projects minimalist luxury.
  • Vectors / Leading Lines: Invisible lines created by arms, roads, or gazes that guide the reader's eye toward the call-to-action or logo.

3. Lighting, Color & Typography

  • Chiaroscuro: Stark contrast between light and dark shadows to dramatize crisis, moral duality, or despair.
  • Color Psychology: Desaturated monochrome (bleakness, history) vs. high-saturation warm tones (urgency, danger, vitality).
  • Serif vs. Sans-Serif: Serif fonts connote institutional tradition and gravitas; bold sans-serif fonts project modern, blunt urgency.

4. Comic & Graphic Conventions

  • Panels & Borders: Rigid rectangular grids suggest regimented monotony; borderless/bleed panels convey emotional overflow.
  • The Gutter: The blank space between panels where the reader's mind performs closure—inferring what happened in the gap.
  • Emanata: Graphic symbols radiating from a character's head (sweat drops, jagged lines, motion blurs) externalizing internal anxiety.
  • McCloud's Transitions: Action-to-action (fast pace), Aspect-to-aspect (lingering mood/setting), Scene-to-scene (temporal jumps).

5. Roland Barthes' Image-Text Interplay: Anchorage vs. Relay

In Paper 1, examiners penalize essays that analyze the image in Paragraph 1 and the text in Paragraph 2 without connecting them. Use Barthes' terminology to analyze their synergy:

Anchorage (Pinning Down Meaning): Images are naturally polysemous (capable of multiple interpretations). Written copy acts as an "anchor" that fixes one specific meaning—often subverting our initial visual assumption through verbal irony.
Relay (Complementary Progression): Text and image work in tandem like partners in a relay race (especially in comics and infographics), where neither is complete alone and both advance the narrative or logical sequence together.

Macro-Essay Organization (Criterion C)

❌ Avoid: Device-Driven Approach (Caps at 3/5)

"Paragraph 1 is about diction. Paragraph 2 is about metaphors. Paragraph 3 is about the image." This leads to repetitive feature-spotting and fragments your argument.

⚠️ Use With Caution: Linear / Structural Approach

Moving chronologically from the beginning/top of the text to the end/bottom. Works well for speeches or comics that undergo a dramatic narrative transformation, provided you still lead with conceptual topic sentences.

✅ Gold Standard: Conceptual / Sub-Argument Approach (Scores 5/5)

Organize paragraphs around 3 distinct sub-arguments that answer the Guiding Question, blending visual, linguistic, and structural devices inside each paragraph. E.g., Para 1: Constructing the crisis; Para 2: Indicting the reader's complicity; Para 3: Offering an empowering path to action.

Micro-Paragraph Architecture: PETAL / What-How-Why

  1. P — Point (Conceptual Topic Sentence): State WHAT argument or effect the author achieves in this aspect of the text, tied directly to the Guiding Question.
  2. E & T — Evidence + Technique (Embedded): Weave short, precise quotations or specific visual details into your own syntax alongside accurate metalanguage (HOW).
  3. A — Analysis of Effect (Unpacking): Zoom in on specific words, connotations, or visual contrasts. Explain WHY this choice manipulates the target audience's thoughts or emotions. Layer at least 2–3 pieces of evidence per paragraph!
  4. L — Link to Purpose & GQ: Synthesize how these combined choices advance the author's overarching persuasive purpose.

The 75-Minute SL Execution Blueprint

Mins 00–05 (Reading Time)
Select & Deconstruct

Read both texts and Guiding Questions carefully. Pick the text where you see the richest interplay of layers (not just the easiest topic). Mentally identify TCAMPS.

Mins 01–15 (Planning)
Color-Code & Outline

Use 3 highlighters—one for each conceptual body paragraph idea. Annotate margins. Write out your full Thesis Statement and 3 topic sentences on scratch paper before starting the essay.

Mins 15–70 (Drafting)
55 Minutes Writing

• Intro (8 mins): 4 sentences (Context/Text-Type, Audience/Purpose, Tone/Message, Thesis answering GQ).
• 3 Body Paras (40 mins): ~13 mins per paragraph.
• Conclusion (7 mins): Synthesize overarching impact.

Mins 70–75 (Polish)
Criterion D Audit

Proofread for grammar, replace vague verbs ("shows", "uses") with precise analytical verbs ("epitomizes", "galvanizes", "subverts"), and verify every paragraph links to the GQ.

Anatomy of a Level 7 Body Paragraph

Compare how visual and linguistic analysis are woven into cohesive PETAL paragraphs that continuously link back to audience and purpose.

Model A: Integrating Visual Salience & Gaze

Multimodal Focus

[Point]To disrupt the viewer's habitual apathy toward wildlife extinction, the charity appeal weaponizes visual pathos and an inescapable demand gaze that implicates the audience in ecological destruction.

[Evidence & Technique]Occupying the center-stage focal point is an extreme close-up of a Sumatran tiger's face, framed in high-contrast chiaroscuro lighting whose amber eyes intersect the upper rule-of-thirds grid line in a piercing, direct gaze.

[Analysis of Effect]By tightly cropping the composition around the animal's face and submerging the encroaching rainforest into pitch-black negative space, the graphic designer visually metaphors the shrinking habitat closing in on the species. Crucially, the tiger's direct 'demand' gaze breaks the fourth wall; rather than appearing predatory, the reflective catchlights in its eyes anthropomorphize the creature, projecting vulnerability and silent accusation. This visual confrontation strips the affluent adult viewer of comfortable detachment, converting aesthetic admiration into acute moral discomfort.

[Link]Consequently, the visual grammar primes the audience emotionally so they are receptive to the financial call-to-action anchored at the bottom of the poster.

Model B: Integrating Diction, Pronouns & Syntax

Linguistic Focus

[Point]Complementing this visual intimacy, the copywriter deploys synthetic personalization and truncated imperative syntax to transform the reader from a passive bystander into the sole arbiter of the species' survival.

[Evidence & Technique]Directly beneath the tiger's chin, stark white sans-serif copy anchors the image: "Their clock is at 11:59. Only YOU can stop the midnight." followed by the isolated imperative, "Act now."

[Analysis of Effect]The apocalyptic metaphor of "11:59" and "midnight" compresses decades of gradual biodiversity loss into a single, ticking minute of kairotic urgency. Furthermore, juxtaposing the helpless third-person plural "Their" against the all-caps second-person pronoun "YOU" enacts synthetic personalization, singling out the individual reader from the mass public. By terminating the copy with the two-word parataxis "Act now," the author eliminates conditional modality, framing financial donation not as an optional charity, but as an immediate ethical imperative.

[Link]Thus, the linguistic urgency locks into the visual pathos to compel immediate donor intervention.
